# SSA Erasure Prevention Principles

Strategic Self-Advocacy treats advocacy, creative work, and lived-experience documentation as authorship.

Minority erasure prevention therefore begins with the author, not the platform, institution, dataset, or AI model.

## 1. Preserve Authorship

Records should identify who authored the material, in what role, and under what authority.

If the author cannot be named publicly, the record should still preserve authorship status, consent limitations, and review authority in protected metadata.

## 2. Preserve Consent Scope

Consent must be specific enough to answer:

1. What can be used?
2. Who can use it?
3. For what purpose?
4. In what format?
5. For what audience?
6. For how long?
7. Can consent be withdrawn?

Missing permission should be treated as no permission.

## 3. Preserve Context

Summaries, translations, reports, datasets, and AI outputs must not remove context that changes meaning.

Context includes disability, culture, language, access needs, family role, service setting, institutional power, geography, socioeconomic conditions, and prior system harm where relevant and consented.

## 4. Preserve Accessibility

Accessibility is part of meaning, not only presentation.

If a person uses Easy Read, AAC, captions, Auslan, plain language, screen-reader structure, or other supports, those supports should be recorded and maintained across publication formats.

## 5. Preserve Human Accountability

AI may assist with drafting, classification, formatting, extraction, translation, or summarisation.

AI must not be the final authority for lived-experience meaning, consent interpretation, erasure risk, publication approval, or community representation.

## 6. Preserve Traceability

Systems should preserve provenance, version history, source relationships, review status, and publication authority.

Traceability makes it possible to see when a record was changed, who changed it, and whether the change affected meaning, consent, accessibility, or authorship.
